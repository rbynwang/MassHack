About Commonwatt

The problem. Massachusetts is electrifying heat and cars, and on hot evenings everyone pulls power at once. Clean power exists, in rooftop solar, home batteries and pre-cooled buildings, but it is scattered across homes, shops and public buildings that each optimize alone, which can create a new, later peak. Grid programs mostly pay battery owners, and joining means handing your data and device control to a utility or aggregator.

Commonwatt. An agent network that turns a neighborhood into an energy commons. Every building gets an agent that knows its owner's private limits. When the grid asks for help, a coordinator negotiates with the agents; each can accept, counter or decline. Trust guards enforce the limits in code and sign receipts. The grid sees one reliable resource.

Why it's credible. The agents call a model trained on Massachusetts data (HouseZero, Boston Public Library, ISO-NE load and prices), replayed on the real June 24, 2025 ISO-NE peak day, with property-based tests on the trust guarantees: no request over an owner's limit, pooled commitments never above what members accepted, payouts that add up, and receipts that cannot be changed without breaking the chain.

Who it serves

Building owners

Households, small businesses, public buildings and large buildings, including those with only a heat pump. They keep their data and limits; they get paid against a signed receipt.

Community stewards

Town utilities (municipal light plants), community groups and housing authorities, who run a commons for their members.

The grid

ISO-NE and distribution utilities, who get one verified resource that answers when asked, instead of thousands of devices to dispatch.
